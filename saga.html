Prologue · Vaikuntha

The Gate

Before there was a demon king, there were two doorkeepers. and guarded the seventh gate of , Vishnu’s own world, which lies beyond all fourteen worlds of the cosmic egg.

One day four travelers came up the road: the . They were sages older than most of creation, but they looked like small boys, and they had already walked through six gates without anyone stopping them.

The doorkeepers saw children. They crossed their maces and barred the way.

The sages didn’t argue. They cursed. The guards had acted out of pride in a world where pride has no place, so they would be born where it does: among the asuras.

Vishnu came to the gate himself. He didn’t undo the curse. He told the two that it would end, and that they would come home.

Versions

Many retellings add a choice: seven births as Vishnu’s devotees, or three as his enemies. The doorkeepers chose three, to come home sooner. The Bhagavata doesn’t include the choice. Narada says only that three births as Vishnu’s enemies, always thinking of him, brought them back.

Bhagavata Purana 3.15–3.16, 7.1

Chapter 1 · The asura capital

The Vow

Their first birth was as twin sons of : and .

Hiranyaksha went looking for a fight with Vishnu, and found one. Vishnu, as the boar, killed him.

Hiranyakashipu took the news like a wound. He bit his lip until it bled, raised his trident, and gave his orders: wherever people worshipped Vishnu, tear it down.

Then he turned to his grieving mother and spoke to her gently about death. Bodies come and go, he told her. The self inside them is never born and never dies.

The Bhagavata gives Hiranyakashipu a long speech on the nature of the self, and it is good philosophy. He knows the teaching. He just won’t apply it to his own grief. That gap between what he knows and what he does drives the whole story.

But alone, he decided something else. Vishnu had taken his brother. So he would make himself into someone nobody could take: not old age, not death, not even Vishnu.

Bhagavata Purana 3.17–3.19, 7.2, 7.3.1

Chapter 2 · The valley of Mount Mandara

The Anthill

He went to a valley at the foot of . He rose onto the tips of his toes, lifted his arms, fixed his eyes on the sky, and stayed there.

Smoke began to rise from his head, and its heat spread through the worlds. Rivers churned, the earth shook, stars fell. In heaven, the gods couldn’t bear it. They went to to complain.

Down in the valley, termites built their mound over him. Grass grew on the mound, and bamboo pushed up through it. Ants ate his flesh.

Underneath, he kept going.

At last Brahma came, riding his swan. He could barely find the man. He sprinkled water from his pot over the anthill, and Hiranyakashipu stood up out of it whole: young, strong, his body shining like molten gold.

Then he asked. He didn’t simply ask never to die. He asked for something more careful: to close, one by one, every door death could come through.

  1. Not by any being Brahma created
  2. Not inside a dwelling, and not outside it
  3. Not by day, and not by night
  4. Not on the ground, and not in the sky
  5. Not by any weapon
  6. Not by human, and not by animal
  7. Not by anything living or non-living
  8. Not by god, demon or great serpent

He also asked for no rival in battle, rule over all beings, and the powers of yoga. Paraphrased from Bhagavata 7.3.35–7.3.38.

Brahma granted all of it.

In these stories, austerity works almost like a law. Do enough of it and the gods are bound to answer, whoever is asking. Brahma grants boons to devas and asuras alike, which is why so many of these stories turn on finding the gap in a boon.

Bhagavata Purana 7.3, 7.4.1–7.4.3

Chapter 3 · Svarga

Three Worlds

With the boon in hand, he took everything: the three worlds, the gods, the guardians of every direction. He moved into ’s palace in heaven and sat on Indra’s throne, and nearly every god had to bow to him.

It went on for a long time. The gods hid, and prayed to Vishnu, and an answer came: a voice with no body.

Wait, it said. When he turns on his own son, a gentle child who hates no one, I will end him, boon or no boon.

That son was . He was kind to everyone, the Bhagavata says, and free of pride, though he was a prince. He was often somewhere else entirely: laughing, crying or singing to himself, lost in thinking about Vishnu.

Bhagavata Purana 7.4

Chapter 4 · The palace school

The Boy Who Wouldn’t Learn

Prahlada’s teachers were Shanda and Amarka, sons of , the asuras’ own guru. They taught him statecraft: how to tell a friend from an enemy, when to bribe and when to punish. He listened politely, and learned something else.

One day his father pulled him onto his lap and asked him for the best thing he had learned.

Prahlada told him the truth: to hear about Vishnu, to sing about him, to remember him, to serve him.

Hiranyakashipu threw the boy off his lap and ordered him killed.

They tried. They stabbed him with tridents. They set elephants and serpents on him, poisoned his food, starved him, left him in the cold, put him in fire and in water, and threw him off a mountain. Sorcerers worked spells on him.

None of it touched him.

While Hiranyakashipu stood in the anthill, Indra raided the asura capital and carried off his pregnant queen, . stopped him, and sheltered her in his hermitage. The child in her womb heard everything Narada taught. Prahlada tells this story himself, later, to his classmates (Bhagavata 7.7).
Versions

The Vishnu Purana (1.17–1.20) spends much longer on these trials, including Prahlada being bound and thrown into the sea.

The trial most people know is : Hiranyakashipu’s sister, who could not be burned, sat in a fire with Prahlada in her lap, and the fire burned her instead. The Bhagavata doesn’t tell it. It comes from later tradition, and it’s why Holi begins with a bonfire.

Bhagavata Purana 7.5, 7.7 · Vishnu Purana 1.17–1.20

Chapter 5 · The great hall

The Pillar

In the end Hiranyakashipu stopped sending other people. He called Prahlada into the great hall and asked him himself. Where does a child get strength like this? Who is protecting you?

“The same one who gives you your strength,” said Prahlada. “And everyone else theirs.”

“Everywhere, is he? Then is he in this pillar?”

“He is,” said the boy.

Hiranyakashipu leapt from his throne, sword in hand, and struck the pillar with his fist.

A sound came out of it that seemed to split the shell of the universe. Far above, in his own world, Brahma heard it and thought the end had come.

And out of the pillar stepped a form that was neither man nor lion.

Bhagavata Purana 7.8.1–7.8.22

Chapter 6 · The doorway, at dusk

The Threshold

Hiranyakashipu fought, and for a while it looked like a fight. caught him, let him slip, and caught him again, the way a predator plays with its prey.

Then he carried him to the doorway of the hall, laid him across his own thighs, and tore him open with his nails.

Versions

Most retellings set this moment at twilight. The Bhagavata’s verse names the doorway, the lap and the nails. The hour comes from commentaries and later tellings.

Chapter 7 · After

The Child and the Lion

It wasn’t over. Narasimha sat on the dead king’s throne, still blazing, and nobody dared come near. Brahma, Shiva and the gods praised him from a distance. Even , the Bhagavata says, had never seen this form, and would not approach.

So Brahma sent the one person who wasn’t afraid. Prahlada walked up, small, and bowed.

Narasimha put his hand on the boy’s head, and the anger went out of him.

Offered any boon he wanted, Prahlada said that someone who serves for rewards is a merchant, not a servant. Then he asked for one thing anyway: that his father be forgiven.

His father was already purified, Narasimha told him, and twenty-one generations of the family with him.

Prahlada became king. And far away, two doorkeepers were one birth closer to home.

Bhagavata Purana 7.8.31–7.10.24
